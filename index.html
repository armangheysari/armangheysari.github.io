Research Statement

What I work on, and why

My research sits at the intersection of distributed systems safety and modern machine learning. Three pillars:

(1) Byzantine fault tolerance meets Byzantine-robust ML. My MSc work and two patents pushed PBFT consensus into System-on-Chip designs — each processing element pairs with a consensus module that validates committed instructions, with execution results reconciled via per-core blockchains and Merkle roots. The same threat model now appears in federated and distributed ML, where malicious workers can poison gradients during training. The defense family (Krum, Bulyan, trimmed mean) mirrors the Byzantine generals problem I worked on in hardware.

(2) Multi-objective optimization for ML systems. During my MSc at Amirkabir University of Technology under Prof. Hamid Reza Zarandi, I used genetic algorithms to tune PoW-based blockchain parameters for security-vs-performance tradeoffs (Elsevier 2025), and NSGA-II for multi-objective optimization of Network-on-Chip reliability and latency under buffer size constraints (Springer 2025). The same constrained, non-differentiable, multi-objective search applies directly to ML hyperparameter optimization, neural architecture search, and hardware-aware distributed training topology design.

(3) Safe offline-to-online reinforcement learning. My next direction is safe offline-to-online reinforcement learning for fault-tolerant distributed systems. The goal is making offline-trained policies safe to deploy online under hard reliability constraints and adversarial fault patterns — combining the formal verification mindset from BFT work with modern deep RL.

Why Byzantine Fault Tolerance Belongs in Hardware
Essay·~5 min read·Posted January 2026
The BFT literature is vast but almost entirely software. Pushing consensus modules into silicon is the missing step for safety-critical SoCs. Where the latency-vs-safety tradeoff actually lives.

Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) and its descendants are usually discussed as distributed-database and permissioned-chain software. The assumption is that nodes are general-purpose machines on a network, and that consensus costs a few extra milliseconds on a request path.

In safety-critical System-on-Chip designs, that framing is wrong. A many-core processor is itself a distributed system: dozens of processing elements (PEs) sharing an interconnect, each running independent firmware, each a candidate for crash or Byzantine misbehavior under transient faults, aging, or adversarial compromise. Treating the SoC as a single trusted monolith is no longer defensible at modern core counts.

In our patent work we pair each PE with a lightweight consensus module that validates every committed instruction via PBFT against a small committee of peer cores. Execution results go to a per-core blockchain and reconcile through Merkle tree roots, so a malicious or faulty PE cannot rewrite history without breaking the root hash its committee observes.

The interesting research question is not whether this works. It does, at modest area overhead. The question is where the latency-vs-safety tradeoff lives. PBFT's three-phase commit adds deterministic rounds; on-chip, those rounds are measured in nanoseconds, not milliseconds, which makes per-instruction consensus feasible. Committee size and reconfiguration policy still dominate tail latency under fault injection. This is where reinforcement learning fits: adaptively choosing committee sizes and reconfiguration thresholds in response to observed fault rates.

Related: Patent No. 140250140003006003 (issued Nov 2023) and PCT/IB2024/061908 (filed Nov 2024).
Genetic Algorithms vs. Gradient-Based Optimization for Blockchain Parameters
Essay·~6 min read·Posted January 2026
Why GA, not gradient descent, is the right tool for tuning discrete, mixed-integer blockchain parameters like block size and interval, and why simulation-based fitness is unavoidable.

When most researchers hear "optimization" they reach for gradients. Stochastic gradient descent, Adam, and their cousins dominate machine learning because the objective is differentiable and the parameter space is continuous. Gradients are very efficient there.

Blockchain parameter tuning violates both assumptions. The decision variables that matter for PoW networks (block size, block interval, and block propagation mechanism) are discrete or mixed-integer. Block size is measured in megabytes; you cannot take a meaningful derivative with respect to it. Block interval is a positive real, but the underlying stochastic process (block propagation, stale rate, selfish-mining resistance) is non-differentiable and only observable through simulation.

Genetic algorithms fit this regime. GA operates on a population of candidate configurations, evaluates each through a simulator (we use NS-3 with an MDP model of the network), and propagates the fittest forward through crossover and mutation. No gradients are required, mixed-integer variables are native, and the fitness landscape (including attack-resilience constraints) can be arbitrarily non-smooth.

The cost is sample efficiency. GA needs many more simulator evaluations than a gradient method would if gradients existed. In our experiments this is acceptable: each NS-3 evaluation is cheap relative to the value of finding configurations that improve both throughput and stale-block rate without opening new attack surfaces.

The deeper point: when the objective is partly about safety (resistance to selfish mining, eclipse attacks), you cannot write a clean scalar objective. You need constrained, multi-objective search, which is exactly what GA (and its cousin NSGA-II, which we used for the NoC work) provides.

Safe RL for Safety-Critical Systems: An Open Problem
Essay·~5 min read·Posted January 2026
The gap between what offline RL can guarantee on paper and what safety-critical infrastructure can tolerate in practice. Why offline-to-online transfer is the right framing.

Reinforcement learning has a proof-of-concept problem in safety-critical systems. The algorithms that are most sample-efficient (online, on-policy) are also the ones most willing to explore unsafe states, and the algorithms that are safest (offline, conservative) are also the ones that generalize least to the distribution shift they face at deployment.

For fault-tolerant distributed systems (the kind my patents target) this is not a theoretical concern. An RL agent that adaptively reconfigures consensus committees around a faulty PE cannot "explore" by intentionally crashing cores in production. It must learn from logs and simulations, then transition safely to online adaptation without ever violating a hard reliability constraint.

The offline-to-online RL literature (conservative Q-learning, uncertainty-aware fine-tuning, recent work on calibrated safe exploration) offers the right vocabulary. But the gap between these guarantees and what a safety-critical system actually requires is enormous. Most current "safe RL" results bound regret relative to a best-policy baseline; safety-critical systems care about absolute violation bounds over the deployment lifetime, with adversarial fault patterns.

My current research interest is exactly this gap: how to take an offline-trained policy for fault-tolerant distributed-system reconfiguration and bring it online with formal guarantees on the number of unsafe actions it may take, ideally zero. The answer probably combines uncertainty estimation (so the agent knows when it is in unfamiliar territory) with the structural safety properties already in the PBFT layer underneath it (so that even a wrong RL decision cannot violate consensus safety, only degrade performance).

If that composition works, distributed systems could learn to be more efficient over time without ever risking the safety properties that make them deployable.

From Byzantine Generals to Byzantine Gradients: Why Hardware Fault Tolerance Matters for Modern ML
Essay·~8 min read·Posted August 2026
The same adversarial model Lamport described in 1982 now shows up in federated learning. A walkthrough from PBFT-in-silicon to Krum and Bulyan, and why my next step is Byzantine-robust safe RL.

Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak, and Marshall Pease posed the Byzantine Generals problem in 1982. The setup is well-known but worth restating because the framing matters. Several divisions of a Byzantine army are camped around a city. Each division has its own general. They must agree on a common battle plan, but communication is by messengers, and one or more of the generals may be a traitor. The traitors can send arbitrary, conflicting messages. The loyal generals need a protocol that lets them agree on a plan regardless of what the traitors do.

The paper proved that the problem is solvable only when loyal generals outnumber traitors by more than three to one. Specifically, in a system of n nodes with at most f Byzantine (arbitrarily malicious) faults, deterministic agreement requires n ≥ 3f + 1. That bound has shaped four decades of distributed systems research.

Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT), introduced by Castro and Liskov in 1999, made the problem practically solvable. PBFT runs in three phases — pre-prepare, prepare, commit — with each non-leader node echoing messages it has seen, so that honest nodes converge on the same value as long as fewer than a third of the participants are Byzantine. The protocol is the foundation of most permissioned blockchain stacks today (Hyperledger Fabric, Tendermint, and so on).

My own work pushed PBFT one layer down. In a many-core System-on-Chip, the individual cores are not a single trusted machine; they are independent processing elements sharing an interconnect, each vulnerable to transient faults, aging, or adversarial compromise. Treating the SoC as one trusted monolith is no longer defensible at modern core counts. The two patents I co-authored pair each processing element with a lightweight consensus module that validates committed instructions via PBFT against a small committee of peer cores. Execution results go to a per-core blockchain and reconcile through Merkle roots, so a faulty or malicious PE cannot rewrite history without breaking the root hash its committee observes. The committee sizes are tuned so the “3f + 1” bound still holds at the granularity of a single instruction.

Now consider how a modern distributed machine learning system is trained. The canonical pattern is a parameter server: many worker nodes compute gradients on different shards of the training data, and a central parameter server aggregates them by averaging. In the more decentralized variant, workers use all-reduce (ring or tree) to average gradients peer-to-peer without a central coordinator. In federated learning, the same averaging happens across edge devices that never share raw data, only model updates.

In all three settings, the central operation is an average of gradients. The natural question: what happens if some of those gradients are wrong?

The answer, derived independently in 2017, is bad. A single malicious worker can send a gradient update that is orders of magnitude larger than the honest average, and the simple arithmetic mean that the parameter server computes will follow that one worker almost entirely. With a carefully crafted malicious gradient, an attacker can steer the model toward a target classification, inject a backdoor, or simply prevent convergence. The attack is not theoretical: it has been demonstrated on real federated learning benchmarks.

The structural insight is that a malicious federated learning worker is a Byzantine general. It can send arbitrary values. The honest workers must agree on a model update despite that. This is the Byzantine Generals problem, restated in terms of gradients instead of orders.

Blanchard et al. recognized this in their 2017 NeurIPS paper, “Machine Learning with Adversaries: Byzantine Tolerant Gradient Descent.” Their algorithm, Krum, replaces the arithmetic mean with a robust aggregation rule. Krum picks, from among the n candidate gradients, the one whose sum of distances to its n − f − 2 nearest neighbors is smallest. Geometrically, this is the gradient that sits closest to a tight cluster of other gradients, which makes it a good representative of the honest majority. Krum is proved to be Byzantine-resilient under the same n ≥ 3f + 1 assumption as classical BFT.

Bulyan, proposed by El Mhamdi et al. at ICML 2018, goes one step further. Krum alone selects a single gradient, which can still carry noise from the selection process. Bulyan runs Krum iteratively to select a subset of n − f honest-looking gradients, then takes a trimmed mean of those. The combination is provably Byzantine-resilient and converges under standard stochastic gradient assumptions. Other robust aggregation rules exist — coordinate-wise median, geometric median, trimmed mean — each with different tradeoffs.

The reason this matters to me is the symmetry. The threat model I have spent years thinking about in hardware — arbitrary faults, adversary-controlled nodes, consensus under partial synchrony — is the same threat model now studied in distributed machine learning. The defenses are different in detail but identical in spirit. PBFT asks the network to agree on a value despite traitors; Krum asks the network to agree on a gradient despite traitors. Both rely on the same quorum bound.

This is the bridge I want to build in my PhD. The natural research question is what happens when the Byzantine-robust ML literature meets the safe RL literature. Most of the Byzantine-robust aggregation work assumes supervised learning, where the loss is a clear, differentiable function of the model. In reinforcement learning, the “gradient” is replaced by a noisy, biased policy gradient, or by a Q-value estimate that depends on the agent’s own trajectory. Byzantine workers in an offline RL setting could poison not just the loss landscape but the trajectory distribution the agent learns from. The defenses — Krum, Bulyan, trimmed mean — were not designed for this regime and may need to be re-derived under the additional structure (and noise) of RL.

The downstream goal is what I would call Byzantine-robust safe RL. An RL agent that learns safely (no unsafe actions during training and deployment) and robustly (its training is not derailed by malicious peers contributing bad trajectories or bad gradients). The combination is, to my knowledge, not yet studied as a single problem. The pieces exist — safe RL on one side, Byzantine-robust ML on the other — and the bridge is exactly the kind of work my hardware BFT background positions me to attempt.

Whether it succeeds is an open question. But the trajectory from a 1982 thought experiment about traitorous generals to a 2017 algorithm that defends neural networks from poisoned gradients is the cleanest example I know of how a piece of distributed systems theory can keep finding new lives, decades after the original problem was solved.
